# Emergence of Anisotropic Defect Networks in Shocked Diamond

**Authors:** Zipeng Xu, Kento Katagiri, Tatiana Pikuz, Wanghui Li, Anirudh Hari, Dorian P. Luccioni, Sophie E. Parsons, Yuichi Inubushi, Kohei Miyanishi, Hirotaka Nakamura, Tadashi Togashi, Makina Yabashi, Toshinori Yabuuchi, Norimasa Ozaki, Leora E. Dresselhaus-Marais

Predicting failure after shock loading requires understanding how the structures created during compression evolve as the material unloads. In diamond, both crystal plasticity and localized amorphization have been reported to accommodate shock-induced shear. However, it remains unresolved how the resulting defect structures organize in three dimensions during compression and how this organization influences where tensile damage localizes during release. Here we couple phase-contrast-enhanced radiography at an X-ray free-electron laser with simulations to address this question in single-crystal diamond. By combining radiographs at multiple pump-probe delays with hydrodynamic calculations, we show that linear features first become visible behind the plastic wavefront while material near the front remains highly compressed. By rotating the viewing direction about the shock axis, we find that the projected feature angles favor finite $\{111\}$ sheets elongated along in-plane directions close to $\langle112\rangle$. Molecular dynamics simulations show that an interconnected network of amorphous bands and stacking faults forms during compression, preferentially on $\{111\}$ variants with larger in-plane shear traction. Projections of this network qualitatively reproduce the observed loss of feature visibility with rotation. During simulated release, tension opens local cracks within these pre-existing amorphous bands. These results support a pathway in which the crystallographically organized defect network formed during compression provides preferred sites for tensile damage during release. This pathway may also operate in other strongly covalent solids.
